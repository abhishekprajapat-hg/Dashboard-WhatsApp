PAPER AND PACKAGING


The following is an India-focused industry audit covering pulp and paper mills, paperboard, cardboard, corrugated boxes, folding cartons, labels, printed packaging, tissue, specialty paper, recycled paper and industrial packaging products. Your source document classifies Paper & Packaging under manufacturing and includes paper mills, cardboard and packaging products.
1. End-to-End Business Flow
The industry operates through several connected value chains:
Paper manufacturing: Fibre sourcing → pulping → stock preparation → paper formation → coating and finishing → converting → distribution
Corrugated packaging: Kraft paper procurement → corrugation → sheet making → printing → cutting and slotting → box conversion → customer delivery
Folding cartons: Paperboard procurement → artwork and structural design → printing → coating → die cutting → folding and gluing → packing
Custom industrial packaging: Customer requirement → packaging engineering → sampling → testing → commercial approval → production → delivery and account retention
Sequential process map
1. Market and demand analysis
Analyse demand from food and beverages, pharmaceuticals, FMCG, e-commerce, electronics, appliances, agriculture, textiles and industrial manufacturing.
Track paper, pulp, recovered fibre, chemicals, ink, energy and freight prices.
Forecast demand by paper grade, board thickness, box style, print requirement, geography and customer segment.
Identify growth opportunities in corrugated packaging, folding cartons, food-grade paper, specialty paper and sustainable alternatives to plastic.
Monitor customers’ product launches, seasonal sales and promotional cycles.
2. Customer acquisition and opportunity generation
Acquire customers through direct B2B sales, industrial clusters, packaging exhibitions, online enquiries, distributors, printers and brand referrals.
Receive packaging enquiries, product dimensions, artwork, target quantities and performance requirements.
Qualify customers based on volume, repeat-order potential, payment terms, technical complexity and expected margin.
Record enquiries, samples, quotations and customer approvals in CRM.
3. Packaging requirement assessment
Understand the product being packed.
Review weight, dimensions, fragility, moisture sensitivity, storage duration and transport conditions.
Determine whether the packaging requires food contact, pharmaceutical, export, cold-chain or hazardous-goods compliance.
Identify stacking, compression, burst-strength and drop-test requirements.
Confirm printing, branding, barcode and labelling expectations.
4. Structural packaging design
Select the appropriate packaging format.
Design carton dimensions, flute type, paper grade, board combination and internal supports.
Determine the required box compression strength.
Prepare CAD drawings and cutting layouts.
Optimise the structure to reduce material without compromising product protection.
Common products include:
Corrugated shipping boxes
Folding cartons
Duplex-board cartons
Paper bags
Paper tubes
Honeycomb boards
Pallets
Edge protectors
Display packaging
Molded-fibre packaging
5. Artwork and graphic development
Receive brand artwork from the customer.
Check dimensions, fonts, colours, barcodes, legal text and print resolution.
Prepare print-ready files.
Create colour references and proof copies.
Obtain customer approval before plate or cylinder production.
6. Sample and prototype development
Produce plain or printed prototypes.
Assemble the packaging around the customer’s product.
Verify dimensions, opening experience, product fit and structural protection.
Perform preliminary drop, stacking, vibration or compression tests.
Modify the design where required.
7. Packaging testing and validation
Testing may include:
Burst-strength testing
Edge-crush testing
Box-compression testing
Cobb or water-absorption testing
Grammage testing
Moisture testing
Drop testing
Vibration testing
Rub resistance
Seal-strength testing
Migration or food-contact testing
The customer approves the final specification after testing.
8. Cost estimation and quotation
Calculate paper, board, ink, adhesive, coating, tooling, printing, conversion, labour, utilities, freight and wastage.
Include expected production setup loss, trim waste and rejection risk.
Calculate cost per unit, thousand units, square metre or tonne.
Define minimum order quantity.
Add freight, taxes and credit cost.
Prepare a quotation with validity period and material-price adjustment terms.
9. Commercial negotiation and order confirmation
Negotiate unit price, quantity, delivery schedule, artwork responsibility, payment terms and rejection conditions.
Confirm whether printing plates, dies or cylinders will be charged separately.
Agree on inventory-holding and call-off arrangements.
Receive purchase order or supply contract.
Collect an advance for tooling or highly customised orders where appropriate.
10. Demand forecasting and production planning
Combine confirmed orders, customer forecasts and historical consumption.
Allocate orders across paper machines, corrugators, printing presses and converting lines.
Group compatible paper grades, colours and board combinations.
Plan maintenance and changeovers.
Reserve capacity for priority customers and seasonal demand.

Paper production may use:
Wood pulp

Recycled fibre
Bagasse
Wheat straw

Fillers

Packaging conversion may require:

Material requirements are calculated from the bill of materials, expected yield and production loss.
12. Supplier sourcing and procurement
Identify domestic and imported sources.
Evaluate paper strength, moisture, grammage, brightness, consistency, recyclability and certification.
Issue requests for quotation.
Negotiate rate contracts, credit and delivery schedules.
Approve backup suppliers for critical grades.
Track supplier performance.
13. Recovered-paper collection and sorting
For recycled-paper mills:
Source recovered paper from waste aggregators, retailers, offices, printers, warehouses and municipal collection systems.
Sort by grade and contamination level.
Remove plastic, metal, glass, food waste and unsuitable material.
Bale the recovered paper.
Record weight, source and quality.
14. Incoming material receipt
Weigh incoming material.
Verify purchase order, supplier, grade and quantity.
Check moisture, contamination and visible damage.
Assign lot numbers.
Place materials in approved, quarantined or rejected status.
15. Incoming quality inspection
Test grammage, moisture, strength, brightness, fibre composition and contamination.
For inks and adhesives, test colour, viscosity, solids or bonding performance.
Compare results against purchase specifications.
Approve or reject the lot.
Update supplier-quality records.
16. Raw-material storage
Store paper rolls, board sheets, recovered fibre, pulp, chemicals and inks.
Protect material from moisture, fire, crushing and contamination.
Track rolls by width, grade, weight and lot.
Apply FIFO or FEFO where applicable.
Maintain separate hazardous-chemical storage where required.
17. Pulping
In a paper mill:
Break down virgin or recovered fibre in water.
Remove contaminants and non-fibre material.
Control consistency, temperature and pulping time.
Transfer pulp to cleaning and screening systems.
Record fibre yield and reject quantities.
18. Cleaning, screening and deinking
For recycled paper:
Remove staples, plastics, sand, adhesives and other contamination.
Use screens, cleaners and flotation systems.
Apply deinking where white or higher-grade paper is required.
Recover acceptable fibre.
Send rejects to approved waste processing.
19. Stock preparation
Refine fibre to achieve bonding and strength.
Blend different fibre sources.
Add fillers, sizing agents, retention aids, dyes and strength chemicals.
Control pulp consistency.
Prepare the final furnish for the paper machine.
20. Paper-sheet formation
Feed diluted pulp onto a moving wire or forming fabric.
Drain water to create a continuous fibre web.
Control basis weight and formation.
Monitor web stability and defects.
Transfer the web to the press section.
21. Pressing and drying
Remove water through mechanical pressing.
Pass the sheet through steam-heated dryer cylinders.
Control moisture, tension and speed.
Recover condensate and heat where possible.
Monitor breaks and production loss.
22. Sizing, coating and surface treatment
Depending on the paper grade:
Apply surface sizing for strength.
Coat the sheet for smoothness, printability or barrier performance.
Add grease, moisture or oxygen barriers.
Control coating weight and uniformity.
Dry and cure the coated surface.
23. Calendaring and finishing
Smooth and compress the paper between rollers.
Achieve the required thickness, gloss and surface quality.
Wind the paper into jumbo rolls.
Inspect the reel for defects.
Record production by grade, machine and batch.
24. Slitting, sheeting and reel conversion
Cut jumbo reels into customer-specific widths.
Convert reels into sheets where required.
Trim edges.
Inspect dimensions and roll quality.
Label each reel or pallet with product and batch information.
25. Corrugation
For corrugated packaging:
Feed liner and fluting paper into the corrugator.
Heat and condition the paper.
Form the fluted medium.
Bond liners using starch adhesive.
Produce single-wall, double-wall or triple-wall board.
Control flute profile, moisture and bonding.
26. Board cutting and creasing
Cut corrugated board into required sheet sizes.
Apply creases for folding.
Maintain dimensional accuracy.
Optimise cutting patterns to minimise trim waste.
Segregate damaged sheets.
27. Printing
Printing methods may include:
Flexographic printing
offset printing
gravure printing
digital printing
screen printing
The process controls:
Artwork version
colour
registration
ink viscosity
print density
barcode readability
drying
28. Lamination and coating
Laminate printed sheets to corrugated board where premium graphics are required.
Apply varnish, aqueous coating or protective film.
Add moisture, grease or scuff resistance.
Inspect bonding and surface finish.
Allow adequate curing time.
29. Die cutting and slotting
Cut carton or box profiles using dies or rotary tooling.
Create slots, handles, windows and locking features.
Remove waste material.
Check cutting accuracy.
Record tooling use and condition.
30. Folding, stitching and gluing
Fold converted blanks into finished packaging.
Apply adhesive, stitching or tape.
Verify joint strength and dimensions.
Bundle flat boxes or assemble them according to customer requirements.
Conduct in-line inspection.
31. Bag, tube and specialty-product conversion
Depending on the manufacturer:
Form paper bags.
Produce tubes and cores.
Manufacture labels and wraps.
Create molded-fibre trays.
Produce honeycomb panels.
Add handles, windows, liners or inserts.
Perform final finishing.
32. In-process quality control
Quality teams inspect:
Grammage
moisture
board caliper
adhesive bond
print registration
colour
dimensions
compression strength
flute formation
cutting and folding accuracy
Non-conforming material is repaired, downgraded, recycled or rejected.
33. Final inspection and release
Confirm customer specification.
Verify quantity and bundle count.
Check artwork, barcode and label accuracy.
Review test results.
Approve or reject the batch.
Prepare a certificate of analysis where required.
34. Finished-goods storage
Store reels, sheets, cartons and boxes by customer and order.
Protect goods from moisture, fire, dust and physical damage.
Track stock by batch, pallet and location.
Monitor customer-owned safety inventory.
Prioritise dispatch based on delivery requirements.
35. Dispatch and logistics planning
Plan vehicles based on volume, weight and loading efficiency.
Avoid crushing and moisture exposure.
Sequence deliveries by geography and customer schedule.
Prepare invoices, e-way bills, packing lists and export documents.
Track shipments and proof of delivery.
36. Customer filling-line support
Packaging suppliers may support customers by:
Testing cartons on automated filling lines.
Adjusting dimensions or crease position.
Solving jamming, sealing or print-readability issues.
Training customer operators.
Reviewing storage and handling practices.
37. Billing and payment collection
Generate invoices after dispatch or delivery.
Reconcile quantities, rates, freight and taxes.
Track credit limits.
Resolve shortage, damage and quality deductions.
Follow up overdue receivables.
Reconcile customer-held inventory and call-off arrangements.
38. Returns and complaint management
Record complaints by order, batch, machine and defect type.
Collect samples and supporting images.
Investigate paper, printing, converting, storage and transport records.
Approve replacement, repair, recycling or credit.
Implement corrective and preventive action.
39. Scrap recovery and recycling
Segregate paper trim, damaged sheets, cores and rejected cartons.
Return usable fibre to the pulping process.
Sell or recycle non-processable waste.
Track recovery percentage and disposal cost.
Prevent mixing with hazardous ink or chemical waste.
40. Customer retention and account growth
Track order frequency, profitability, rejection rate and service level.
Offer packaging redesign and material reduction.
Maintain vendor-managed inventory.
Introduce sustainable or premium packaging alternatives.
Support new product launches.
Negotiate long-term rate and supply agreements.

5. Future Growth Prospects
Revenue trends
India’s paper and packaging market has a favourable medium-term outlook, driven by e-commerce, packaged food, pharmaceuticals, consumer goods and organised retail. IBEF reports that India’s total packaging industry was worth approximately US$84 billion in 2024 and projects it could reach around US$143 billion by 2029, although different market studies use different definitions and therefore produce different forecasts. A separate 2026 estimate cited by IBEF projects the broader packaging industry at approximately US$92 billion by FY2030, growing at around 9% annually. (IBEF)
India’s domestic paper market was estimated at approximately 23.84 million tonnes in FY2024–25, with packaging paper and paperboard contributing about 15.54 million tonnes. Production is expected to rise toward 35 million tonnes by FY2030, supported by packaging and tissue demand. (IBEF)

E-commerce and quick-commerce shipments

Sustainability and regulatory outlook
Sustainability will be a central commercial driver rather than only a compliance issue.
Customers will increasingly request:
Recycled content

reduced plastic lamination
water-based inks

IBEF reports that Extended Producer Responsibility requirements for paper packaging introduce phased recycling targets beginning from April 2026, creating greater need for material tracking, recovery evidence and compliance reporting. (IBEF)
The shift away from selected single-use plastic formats also creates opportunities for paper bags, moulded fibre, cartons and coated paper. However, paper is not automatically more sustainable in every application. The environmental outcome depends on fibre source, material weight, coatings, recyclability, production energy and transport efficiency.
Investment and consolidation outlook
The industry is attracting investment in integrated mills, corrugated packaging, specialty paper and aseptic cartons. India has more than 22,000 packaging units, with SMEs accounting for the majority, and hundreds of paper mills serving a highly fragmented market. This fragmentation creates opportunity for consolidation, modernisation and shared technology platforms. (IBEF)
Recent expansion has included new corrugated facilities, specialty-paper lines, aseptic carton production and acquisitions by established paper groups. This indicates increasing demand for scale, modern equipment and closer integration between board production and packaging conversion. (IBEF)
